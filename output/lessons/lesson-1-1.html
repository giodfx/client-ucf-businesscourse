Choosing Your U.S. Business Entity

Choosing Your Entity: The First Decision

Before you file anything in Florida, you need to make one key decision: what kind of business entity to form. In Central Florida, the process starts online through a portal called SunBiz.

An LLC gives you flexibility and low overhead. A C-Corp gives you the structure investors expect. Both protect you from personal liability. But they work very differently when it comes to taxes, visas, and raising money.

This lesson walks you through the two main options that work for international founders, and one that usually does not.

Did You Know?

Florida has no state personal income tax, but that is not the only reason founders keep choosing it. Central Florida has over 30,000 businesses started by foreign-born owners (Florida Chamber Foundation, 2024), and the state processes new LLC filings faster than most others. Some business owners have gone from zero to an active entity in under 48 hours.

Three Structures, Three Different Paths

An LLC is simple to set up, easy to run, and it keeps your personal assets separate from the business. A C-Corp requires more paperwork and formality, but it is the structure investors expect if you plan to raise venture capital. An S-Corp exists too, but it requires all shareholders to be U.S. citizens or residents, which rules out many international founders.

How Your Entity Type Connects to Everything Else

Your entity choice creates a chain reaction across banking, taxes, visas, and fundraising. Here is how the pieces connect:

Banking: Having your articles of organization, EIN, and operating agreement ready before you visit a bank increases your chances of approval. LLCs and C-Corps require different documentation at the bank. We cover this in detail in Lesson 3 of this module.

Taxes: LLC profits pass through to your personal return, with specific IRS requirements for non-resident aliens. A C-Corp files its own return (Form 1120). Each structure has different tax obligations. Module 2 goes deeper.

Visas: Immigration lawyers often prefer C-Corps for E-2 treaty investor visa cases. If a visa is part of your plan, talk to a lawyer before choosing your entity.

Investors: U.S. venture capital firms usually require a Delaware C-Corp. Starting with the right entity avoids this.

Real Case: Wrong Entity, Costly Switch

A software founder from India chose a Florida LLC because it was fast and cheap. Eight months later, a U.S. investor offered to lead her seed round but required a Delaware C-Corp. The switch took several months and significant legal fees, and nearly derailed the deal.

Common Mistakes International Founders Make

  • Choosing the wrong entity type for your visa or tax situation. A founder who needs an E-2 visa but formed a single-member LLC may need to rebuild the structure before the visa process can move forward. That means legal fees, delays, and stress that could have been avoided.
  • Using personal bank accounts for business transactions. This is called "piercing the corporate veil." It can destroy the liability protection your entity provides. If you mix personal and business funds, a court can hold you personally responsible for business debts. Keep them separate from day one.
  • Ignoring the registered agent requirement. Florida law requires every business entity to have a registered agent with a physical Florida address. If your registered agent lapses, the state can dissolve your entity, meaning your business legally stops existing until you fix it.

Watch Your Money: What This Actually Costs

Entity formation costs vary depending on the type of entity, whether you use an attorney, and which state you register in. Here are typical ranges:

  • Florida LLC: Around $125 in state filing fees, plus $0–$500 if you use an attorney. Total typically $125–$625.
  • Florida Corporation (C-Corp): Around $70 in state filing fees, plus $300–$1,500 for attorney assistance with articles, bylaws, and stock issuance.
  • Delaware C-Corp (common for investors): Around $89 in state filing, plus annual franchise tax of $300–$500, plus attorney fees. You also need to foreign-qualify in Florida if you operate there. First-year costs can range from around $960–$2,600 or more.
  • Registered agent service: Typically $100–$300 per year.
  • EIN application: Free from the IRS.

The most affordable path is often a Florida LLC filed on your own. A common investor-ready path is a Delaware C-Corp with an attorney. Choose based on your business needs, not just the price tag.

Check Your Understanding

What Would You Do?

Question 1 of 3

You are an international founder planning to raise venture capital within the next 18 months. You are about to register your first U.S. entity. How should you approach this?

U.S. venture capital firms generally require a C-Corp (often a Delaware C-Corp). Starting with the right structure avoids a costly conversion later. The S-Corp is generally not available to non-resident alien entrepreneurs.

A fellow founder tells you: 'Just form an LLC like I did. It worked for me.' How should you evaluate this advice?

Every founder's situation is different. What works for a freelance consultant from Canada may not work for a tech startup founder from the UK who plans to raise capital. Entity choice depends on your visa status, tax treaty, industry, and growth plans. Blindly following another founder's path ignores these differences, while dismissing peer advice entirely or forming two entities wastes time and money.

You are forming a Florida LLC as an international founder. You do not live in the United States and have no physical U.S. address. Which of the following is required by Florida law?

Florida law requires every business entity to have a registered agent with a physical Florida address. This is not optional, if your registered agent lapses, the state can dissolve your entity. You do not need a bank account, SSN, or attorney to file with SunBiz, though all can be helpful. Commercial registered agent services are available for $50–$200 per year.

Key Takeaway

Your entity type is the foundation of your U.S. business. It affects taxes, banking, visas, and whether investors will work with you. For many international founders, the real choice is between an LLC (simple, flexible, affordable) and a C-Corp (investor-ready, more structure). The S-Corp is generally not available to non-citizen founders. Whether you choose an LLC or a C-Corp, that choice shapes every step that follows.

Don't default to the cheapest option without knowing how it connects to your bigger picture. If your situation involves visa concerns, future investors, or home-country tax treaties, think about meeting with a business attorney before you file anything. A one-hour meeting may cost a few hundred dollars. Fixing the wrong entity choice later can cost several thousand, plus months of delays.
